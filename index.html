Diwali Doesn’t Arrive Unexpectedly. Should Delivery Failures?

India’s e-commerce boom is accelerating. Is its last-mile supply chain keeping up? A consultant’s read of the evidence, kept honest about what is known and what is not.

The question

If festive demand is predictable, why do delivery promises still break?

Every year the festive peak is on the calendar months ahead. Yet every year, somewhere between checkout and doorstep, a promised date slips. Like many households, mine had its share of late parcels this Diwali. This note is not about any one retailer or courier. It is about the system underneath, and what the data says about it.

A customer does not judge a network against last year’s average. They judge it against the date promised at checkout. A network can get faster and still disappoint more people if the promises grow bolder than the operations behind them.

Why it matters

The promise gap

Think of delivery performance as two numbers. One is what the network actually achieves. The other is what the customer was promised at checkout. Customer disappointment lives in the gap between them. When retailers compete on speed, the promise can move faster than the operation underneath, and the gap widens even while the network improves.

That is why “average delivery time fell” and “my parcel is late” can both be true on the same day. Averages hide the long tail, and customers live in the tail. A parcel that takes six days instead of two is not offset, in the customer’s mind, by a thousand parcels that arrived early.

Three questions are worth asking of any network. Is absolute performance improving? Is the improvement even across cities and lanes? Is the delivery promise becoming more aggressive faster than reliability is improving? The third question is the one the industry reports least often, and probably the one that matters most.

Where it breaks

Six stages of festive failure (tap to light up)

Demand forecasting and capacity

Plans built on monthly averages, not peak-day, PIN-code level demand. KPI: Delivery Promise Accuracy.

Seller and warehouse readiness

Late dispatch and packing quality set up everything downstream. KPI: dispatch SLA, Transit Damage Rate.

Middle-mile and hub congestion

Linehaul and sorting backlogs build quietly. KPI: Hub Dwell Time.

Sortation and parcel handling

Mis-sorts, rough handling and damage. KPI: Transit Damage Rate.

Last-mile workforce and execution

Hiring, training, route density and incentives. KPI: First-Attempt Delivery Success.

Exception management and recovery

What happens once something goes wrong. KPI: NDR rate, Exception Resolution Time.

Delivery-attempt integrity

A failed attempt should be provable

Payment and proof of handover are different things, so a one-time password at the door is legitimate even for prepaid orders. The real question is whether the recorded reason for a failed delivery matches what happened. Picture it: the customer has paid, the parcel has not arrived, and somehow the customer is the one explaining why a code was never shared. Somewhere, the process got turned upside down.

What research says. A peer-reviewed study in Management Science (2025, Arora, Choudhary and Kireyev, using data from an Indian last-mile firm) examines “fake remarked deliveries”, where an agent records a customer as unavailable without reaching the address. It finds the losses spill into the next day, lowering overall delivery success by about 1.5%.
Verified: journal abstract, DOI 10.1287/mnsc.2023.01829

This is evidence that the problem is measurable. It does not show how widespread it is, or that any specific delivery was mis-recorded.

Frontline pressure matters too. Festive hiring is intense and attrition is high, so incentives and exception design deserve as much scrutiny as individual behaviour.

Fair counterpoints

The case that things are improving

  • Smaller cities posted better on-time delivery than metros in the 2026 D2C sample.
  • Networks are investing in automation, peak staffing and capacity. Company-stated plans (for example peak front-end staffing increases) are intentions, not outcomes.
  • Scale and reliability are different muscles. Moving more parcels is real progress, and still not the same as keeping every promise.
What should change

Recommendations

Marketplaces and brands

Recompute delivery promises from live network conditions. Use PIN-code carrier scorecards and dynamic allocation.

Logistics providers

Verifiable attempt protocols (location, timestamp, proof). Plan peak labour from peak-day workload.

Last-mile operations

Audit exception patterns, strengthen fragile handling, fix root causes of recurring transit damage.

Governance

Clear SLAs, escalation paths, independent review of disputed attempts, and published service indicators.

Diwali doesn’t arrive unexpectedly. The shopping season doesn’t arrive unexpectedly. The demand surge doesn’t arrive unexpectedly. Perhaps it is time for our delivery commitments to stop behaving as though they do.
